Perceived performance

The perception of performance is largely determined by four factors: duration, responsiveness, fluency and tolerance.

Duration · Responsiveness · Fluency · Tolerance · Other factors

Duration

Duration is the actual time a process takes. This is what technical discussions usually call “performance”. Different magnitudes of duration require different treatments to achieve an optimal perceived performance.

TermDurationResponse
Instantaneousup to 100 msAcknowledge user input within this time frame. Ideally, a visible process towards completing the task begins within this time span.
Immediate500–1000 msAnswers to simple requests must be completed within this time frame (e.g. opening a new window).
Continuous2000–5000 msAnswers to complex requests must be completed within this time frame (e.g. loading a web page).
Captive7000–10000 msUsers will begin switching tasks at this point. If a process takes longer, it should be segmented.

Research notes

  • Nielsen Norman Group gives three limits: 0.1 s feels instantaneous, 1 s keeps the train of thought unbroken, 10 s is the limit for holding attention.
  • Google’s RAIL model likewise aims to respond to input within 100 ms.

Responsiveness

Responsiveness is the perceived time it takes the system to respond to user input.

Guideline: React to input right away, even if the full result takes longer. An empty dialog that appears immediately after a click and fills with content a second later feels faster than the same dialog appearing fully populated after a one second delay.

Click the button in both demos. The content takes one second in each.

Research notes

  • Interaction to Next Paint, a current web metric, rates 200 ms or less from interaction to the next painted frame as good, and above 500 ms as poor.

Fluency

Fluency is the perceived smoothness of a process. It can also be described as a measure of how hard the machine appears to be working.

Guideline: Aim for a constant 60 frames per second. A stuttering progress indicator gives the impression of lower performance, regardless of the actual duration. If smooth animation is not possible, prefer a static state over a stuttering one.

The same rule applies to transitions: a panel should open with a fluid animation, or without any animation if the hardware cannot render it smoothly. A demo for this is prepared but still empty.

Research notes

  • At 60 frames per second a frame lasts about 16 ms. RAIL suggests producing each animation frame in 10 ms or less, to leave the browser time to render.
  • The “don’t” demo changes speed as well as smoothness, so it does not isolate smoothness alone.

Tolerance

Tolerance is a measure of how long the user expects a process to take, and at what point they will abandon or cancel it.

Guideline: Match duration to the perceived size of the task. The tolerated duration for loading a web page is much longer than for saving a bookmark.

Both bars are identical and take about 30 seconds. Only the task differs.

Research notes

  • At about 30 seconds, even the “do” case exceeds the captive range above. A real process of this length should be segmented.
  • Progress bar research from Carnegie Mellon University reports that stripes moving backwards make a wait feel shorter, as used in both bars here, and that pauses are more tolerable early in a process than near the end.

Other factors

Bias and priming: A user’s preconception about the speed of something, based on brand or prior knowledge, influences the perception of time. For example, users perceived a browser as faster after reading an article about its recently improved performance.

These can also influence the perception of performance:

  • Perceived task complexity
  • Emotional state
  • UI complexity and visual noise
  • Trends
  • Task frequency, attempts, failures

Further reading: Why Performance Matters by Denys Mishunov (part 1, part 2, part 3) and Designing and Engineering Time by Steven C. Seow.
